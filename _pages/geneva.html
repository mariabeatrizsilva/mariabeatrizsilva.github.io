Text-to-video models can render a convincing scene, then add a hand that belongs to no one or make steam appear from nowhere. GeneVA records where, when and what those artifacts are, as judged by people.

Abstract

Recent advances in probabilistic generative models have extended capabilities from static image synthesis to text-driven video generation. However, the inherent randomness of their generation process can lead to unpredictable artifacts, such as impossible physics and temporal inconsistency. Progress in addressing these challenges requires systematic benchmarks, yet existing datasets primarily focus on generative images due to the unique spatio-temporal complexities of videos. To bridge this gap, we introduce GeneVA, a large-scale artifact dataset with rich human annotations that focuses on spatio-temporal artifacts in videos generated from natural text prompts. We hope GeneVA can enable and assist critical applications, such as benchmarking model performance and improving generative video quality.

The dataset

We sampled real prompts that people wrote for text-to-video tools (from VidProM) and generated videos from each one with three models: Sora, Pika and VideoCrafter2. That gives about 14.5 hours of content in total. Crowd workers rated each video for overall quality and for how well it matched its prompt, both on 7-point scales. They then marked up to five artifacts per video, each with a category, a free-text description, and a bounding box tracked across the frames where the artifact appears.

What the annotations show

1

Models differ, and people notice

Sora was rated well above Pika and VideoCrafter2 on both quality and prompt alignment. Its videos also had fewer artifacts, which covered less of the frame. Ratings for the other two models clustered around the middle of the scale.

2

Missing the prompt costs quality too

When a video didn’t match its prompt, annotators lowered not only the alignment score but also the overall quality score, for every model. Mismatched videos also had more artifacts.

3

More artifact area, lower quality

The more of the frame that artifact boxes covered, the lower people rated a video. The correlation is weak but highly significant (pooled r = −0.26, p < 0.0001). Coverage explains only part of the rating, which is one reason GeneVA also records what each artifact is.

A baseline artifact detector

To show what the dataset enables, we trained a two-stage model that finds artifacts and describes them in words. A temporal fusion module attends across five frames around an anchor frame. A transformer detector (RT-DETR) then localizes artifacts, and a captioning model (GIT) writes a short description of each one.

The scores are low in absolute terms. Artifacts are hard to localize precisely because they rarely have the clean edges of ordinary objects, and that difficulty is part of what makes GeneVA a useful benchmark. Even so, the detector does far better than chance, and looking across time clearly helps. It also transfers to a model it never saw in training: on videos from Google’s Veo, it finds and describes plausible artifacts.

My role

I built the data acquisition pipeline: a Python system that located and extracted the study videos from a 2.7 TB generated-video corpus within HPC storage limits. I also analyzed generated videos and reviewed prior work to shape the artifact taxonomy. During study design, I proposed showing annotators each video’s prompt, which is what lets GeneVA measure prompt–video alignment as well as visual artifacts.
